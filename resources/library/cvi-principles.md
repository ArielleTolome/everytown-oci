# CVI principles: the foundation under every domain

This guide holds the shared ideas that the other field guides build on. Use it to keep recommendations grounded in how community violence intervention (CVI) actually works.

## What CVI is

- CVI uses evidence-informed, community-centered strategies to prevent and disrupt cycles of violence and retaliation, and to connect people to services that save lives, address trauma and build opportunity (BJA CVI Implementation Checklist, Introduction).
- CVI is a local ecosystem of strategies, people and coordinated systems, not a single program. It is focused on the people and places at highest near-term risk, relationship-based and public-health-informed (Dr. Tillmon, What Is CVI, slide 3).
- Serious violence is concentrated among a small number of people, places and social networks, so targeted, relationship-based strategies are most likely to make a difference (Dr. Roman, Why CVI).
- A shooting, hospitalization, arrest or threat can open a short window when someone is more ready to accept help. The work is to step in and then stay in, long after the crisis passes (Dr. Roman, Why CVI; HVIP focus group brief, 2026).

## Guiding principles

The BJA checklist names four principles that work well as a test for any plan or recommendation (BJA CVI Implementation Checklist, Guiding Principles):

1. **Community-centered:** shaped by residents, with the community's needs first.
2. **Equitable and inclusive:** the people most affected help create the solutions.
3. **Evidence-informed:** built on research, case studies and lessons from the field.
4. **Effective and sustainable:** measurable impact, with resources to keep going over time.

## People and trust

- Credible messengers hold a "License to Operate": earned permission to enter high-risk networks and stay trusted enough to support change. Lived experience may open the door; competence, integrity, consistency and results keep it open (Dr. Tillmon, slide 6).
- Staff with lived experience and community credibility build trust faster than credentials alone (HVIP focus group brief, 2026).
- Community members are co-creators and leaders of safety, not passive recipients (Dr. Tillmon, slide 12).
- Trauma-informed approaches reduce the chance of retraumatizing people and promote safety, trust, empowerment and healing (BJA Checklist, Step 4.3).

## Support built around the person

- Wraparound services should be organized around the participant, not the program menu. Ask what this person needs to become safer and more stable: housing, behavioral health, education, employment, legal help, family support, health care and mentoring (Dr. Tillmon, slide 7).
- Practical help such as transportation and accompaniment often decides whether someone actually gets care (HVIP focus group brief, 2026).

## Roles and boundaries

- The ecosystem works through coordination, trust and role clarity, not duplication or competition (Dr. Tillmon, slide 8).
- CVI and law enforcement can share a safety goal without sharing a function. Clear boundaries protect the trust that makes intervention possible (Dr. Tillmon, slide 9; BJA Checklist, Step 4.2).

## Why organizational capacity matters

Leadership, supervision, data systems, training, succession planning and mid-level roles are not side tasks. They are part of the infrastructure that keeps frontline services strong (HVIP focus group brief, 2026, implications for funders). This is the reason the OCI exists.

## How to talk about OCI results

- The OCI is a learning experience, not an evaluation. It reflects organizational maturity, not performance, and there is no external grading (OCI handoff deck, 2025).
- Lead with stages and growth: Seeding, Emerging, Developing, Strengthening, Transformational. Treat gaps as growth priorities.
- Reflection and team conversation create the value, so frame next steps as things a leadership team can discuss and own together (OCI handoff deck, 2025).
