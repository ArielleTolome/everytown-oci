# Governance (GV) field guide

Governance is how a board of directors or advisory board guides and oversees the organization: structure, meetings, fiduciary oversight, the mix of skills and community voices, and leadership succession. In the 2025 cohort, Governance showed the widest spread of any domain, which means organizations experience their boards very differently (2025 OCI cohort findings). Good governance is learnable, and small steps add up.

## By stage

### Seeding and Emerging: form the group and set the basics

- Bring together a governing or advisory body that meets on a regular schedule with a simple, consistent agenda (OCI questionnaire, Governance).
- Put bylaws and roles in writing. A clear mission, adopted and reviewed by the board, guides every decision (IRS, Governance and Related Topics).
- Include people with lived experience and residents of the communities you serve. Community representation keeps discussions focused on real priorities and service gaps (HVIP focus group brief, 2026).

### Developing: share the work of leading

- Ask the board to help set strategy, policies and priorities, and to take part in resource development (OCI questionnaire, Governance).
- Keep written minutes of key decisions and make them easy to find (OCI questionnaire, Governance).
- Adopt a few core policies, such as conflict of interest, whistleblower protection and document retention (IRS, Governance and Related Topics).
- Model the ethics of the field: confidentiality, boundaries, accountability, dignity and respect for lived expertise (Dr. Tillmon, slide 10).

### Strengthening and Transformational: oversight, succession and voice

- Have the board review and approve the annual budget and financial reports, and review the Form 990 before filing (IRS, Governance and Related Topics).
- Define board terms, form committees for key areas such as finance and fundraising, and recruit new members on purpose (OCI questionnaire, Governance).
- Keep a clear line between governance and day-to-day operations, and evaluate the executive director each year.
- Write a succession plan. Relying on one champion leaves a program exposed when leaders change; build relationships across many people and offices (HVIP focus group brief, 2026).
- Join or help form coalitions. A statewide coalition can be the collective face during controversy or advocacy, offering protection a single program may not have (HVIP focus group brief, 2026).

## Practical tips

- Use a cross-sector advisory group if a full board is not yet realistic. Leaders found advisory groups helpful for sharing information and reducing duplication, and they adapted models from peer programs (HVIP focus group brief, 2026).
- Give new members a short orientation on CVI, trauma-informed practice and the board's legal duties.
- Make it easy to serve: predictable meeting times, stipends or travel support where possible, and plain-language materials.

## Team conversation starters

- Does our board reflect the community we serve?
- If our executive director left tomorrow, who would lead, and how?
- What decisions should the board own, and which belong to staff?
